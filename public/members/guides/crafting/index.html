This page is built from the Blades' own advice in #crafting (March 2024 to November 2026). It is what worked for people in a garage, not a professional manual, so treat brands and prices as starting points and follow the maker's own directions on any dye, paint, or finish. Stuck? Post a photo in #crafting. Someone has almost certainly solved it already. Looking to buy rather than make? See Shopping.

Quick Answers

What dye for blue and yellow cloth?

Jacquard iDye (Brilliant Blue and Golden Yellow) or Rit All-Purpose. Blue is the hard one. Details.

What dye for leather?

Fiebing's or Angelus leather dye, then buff and seal. Test on scrap first. Details.

Do I have to match the colors exactly?

No. Blue and yellow in your own shades is the standard, and a small piece of color is enough. Details.

How do I paint armor without rust?

Degrease, prime with a metal primer, use enamel, and avoid water-based paint. Details.

What do I use to build a shield?

Corrugated plastic core, foam on both faces, pipe insulation on the edge. Details.

Where do I get wool?

It is hard and pricey. Felt is the budget answer for slashed outerwear. Details.

What leather and tools do I start with?

7 to 9 oz veg tan, a knife, beveler, burnisher, punch, awl, thread, rivets. Details.

Where do I find a pattern?

Reconstructing History is the most-used source. Expect some pain. Details.

Armor & Metal

"I bought a blackened or oily breastplate. How do I paint it?"

  • Wipe it down first with odorless mineral spirits or WD-40 and shop towels to remove the oil.
  • Prime with a spray primer made for metal. Blades use automotive primer and Rust-Oleum Stops Rust primer as the base coat on the whole piece.
  • Prime the inside too. Rust blooms on the inside ruin the clothing you wear under armor.
  • Avoid water-based paint and cheap acrylics directly on bare metal. The water in the binder can rust the metal underneath, and one Blade had to redo a set of pieces after exactly that. If you do want acrylics for detail, seal the metal with primer or enamel first.
  • Enamel spray paint, automotive enamel, and oil paints all work. Oils were chosen by one Blade specifically to avoid rust.
  • Clear coat last, in several thin coats like a car, then polish if you want a smooth finish.

Cutting and shaping

  • Rolling edges is more historical and looks and feels better than a cut edge, but it can be tricky on thick metal. Armorers roll over a wire to keep the shape and apply heat if the metal is thick.
  • Cutting, filing, sanding, and rounding the edges is acceptable and was common historically. An angle grinder helps with trimming a piece that is too long or wide for you.

Gold, silver, and special effects

  • Montana chrome gold spray paint is a Blade favorite for realistic gold. It is not a primer, so prime first, and clear coat if the piece will take hits.
  • Gold leaf on metal can be sealed with linseed oil and then a thin protective sealant. Use a stencil for the glue.
  • Rub and buff finishes were discussed for foam and plasti-dip. Test on scrap before committing.
  • Stencils are the easy way to put the same marking on several metal pieces, such as matching helmets.

3D-printed armor

  • PLA does not sand smooth easily. One Blade's sequence was filler (bondo, thinned with mineral spirits), sanding, sandable primer, sanding, then regular primer.
  • Blades are curious about tough printed plastics (ABS) as a lighter alternative to metal, but nobody has reported a tested result yet. Printed armor is not from an approved vendor, so check with weapons check staff before you rely on it.

Dyes & Colors

Do I need to match the Golden Blade colors exactly?

No. Every Blade handles their own gear, and the goal is general representation, not an exact match. Some prefer a cool yellow and some a warm one, some a navy blue and some an ultramarine. Wear what makes you happy, and you only need a small piece of the colors somewhere on your kit. The official heraldry and flag files are in the Heraldry & Flags folder if you want to see the artwork, and the Primer covers what is expected.

"What dye should I use on cloth?"

Jacquard iDye

A Blade used Brilliant Blue and Golden Yellow for the House colors on cloth. Powder form. Blades who dye regularly like it.

Rit All-Purpose

Widely available at Michaels and similar stores, in liquid and powder. Blades like the powder in the golden shade for yellow. One yellow hat was dyed with a mix of Rit powder and liquid. Rit's own website has detailed instructions, and Blades say to follow them exactly. Here is a video guide to Rit liquid dye.

Rit Color Remover

Blades say this works well when a dye job goes wrong. Keep some on hand when you try a new color.

Tips from Blades who have dyed a lot

  • You can always go darker, but not lighter. Start light and repeat if needed.
  • Blue is the hard color. Several Blades fought it. A "royal blue" can come out as light blue. One Blade needed three rounds across two dye packs, and a first result that looked right wet dried back to the old pale color. Higher dye concentration (a double pack) fixed it. A navy tunic from light blue worked by dyeing it again. Expect to experiment.
  • Judge color when dry. A cap that went from beige to navy or indigo in the bath looked different once it dried.
  • Use a fixative (a dye setter) and wash more than once. It reduces bleeding. A dye-setting product was credited by one experienced dyer for preventing color from bleeding in the wash.
  • Prewash new fabric before dyeing or sewing, to prevent both shrinking and bleeding.
  • Natural fibers are the easy case. Cotton and linen take dye well. Synthetics need a sustained high temperature on the stovetop, so do not expect a washing machine or a lukewarm bath to work.
  • Check for dye-friendly fabric before you buy. A shop that sells fabric meant for dyeing, such as Apparatus LARP (see Shopping), saves trouble. Buying white or natural garb and dyeing it is a common route to House colors.
  • Use a big pot or tub. A tub works, and so does a very large cooking pot (one Blade lends out a 32 quart tamale pot). Do not reuse food pots for dyeing without cleaning them thoroughly.
  • Be patient with thick things. Dyed rope takes about three days in the sun to dry fully.
  • Felt differs by color. Blades found yellow felt noticeably flimsier than the blue felt from the same maker, and a felt supplier was able to explain why when asked.
  • Mixing dye and airbrushing. Some Blades airbrush dye on for speed and even coverage. If you do, use a respirator.

"What dye do I use on leather?"

Leather dye is a different product from fabric dye. The details are in Leatherwork, but the quick version:

  • Angelus leather dye in yellow is rich and deep, and Blades call it the closest thing to an official yellow. It is sold on Amazon and at leather suppliers.
  • Fiebing's dyes are the other common pick. Fiebing's purple is beautiful but dilute it with alcohol, because it can leave a metallic residue that never goes away.
  • Test on scrap from the same hide. The same dye mix came out a different shade on a 4 to 6 oz piece and a 9 to 10 oz piece in one Blade's test. Even one hide varies.
  • Light colors like pale blue and bright yellow are easy to overshoot. One Blade found a "Lex" color easy to take too dark too quickly.

Fabric & Wool

"Where do I get wool or felt?"

The honest answer from the Blades is that real wool is hard to find and expensive: roughly $40 to $50 a yard locally, with poor color choice, especially since Joann closed. These are the workarounds.

  • Felt is the cheap alternative and a good fit for slashed outerwear, because cut felt does not fray and does not need hemming. It is harder to work with than wool and flimsier in some colors.
  • Woolsome (Poland) sells handwoven wool twill and plain weave in a full color range for about $43 to $48 a yard, the cheapest one Blade has found. Handwoven means a tight weave that resists fraying. It ships by FedEx only, and a free sample got stuck in customs for a while, so order early.
  • Renaissance Fabrics sells lightweight tabby wool at about $28 a yard (2025 price).
  • The Historical Fabric Store has a large selection but is aimed at serious reenactors. Tabby wool was about $50 a meter.
  • Nick of Time Textiles (Pennsylvania) is a wholesale shop with good prices on linen, cotton, and twill, though little wool in House colors. A Blade there got 7 yards of muslin for $28.
  • Local places: thrift stores and estate-sale shops can turn up fabric cheaply, Michaels is slowly absorbing Joann's stock, and a local fabric store can often order what they do not stock. Quilting stores are a good source for cotton.
  • Destash sellers such as Destash.us sell leftover bolts and quilted cotton.
  • Fabrics-store.com and wholesale sites carry linen options if you are not set on wool.

Linen, canvas, and fraying

  • Linen is light and good for hot events, but it frays a lot and thin linen can look "cartoonish". Look for a heavier weight.
  • Canvas or denim is the usual answer when you need something sturdy and cheap, such as a backing.
  • Synthetic satin edges can be sealed with a lighter. Do this carefully and only on synthetics.
  • Make slashes before sewing the garment together. One Blade learned this the hard way, and matching slashes across pieces is stressful.

Foam, Shields & Pavise

Weapons and shields are checked at the event. Blades report that shields are generally passed, but not every shield is safe. The main thing is that nothing, whether metal bracing, screws, or the handle, can punch through the plastic or foam. Homemade weapons do not pass weapons check at Hynafol. See Shopping, Weapons for the full picture.

"What do I build a shield from?"

  • The common recipe: corrugated plastic for the structure, covered in dense foam, with pipe or hose insulation on the edges, then painted. This is how many Lodge shields are made.
  • A stiffer version: sandwich two corrugated plastic boards at 90 degrees so the ribs of each layer run across the other, with foam mats on both sides. Blades glue with E6000 (Gorilla Glue also works).
  • Keep the edging strip of the foam mats separate and use it to make an outer foam ring around the shield, so the hard plastic never meets an opponent's weapon.
  • Make sure the shield has a core so it is not floppy. Plaster cloth was floated as extra rigidity, though no one has reported a result yet.
  • DIY lets you cut pike notches, put sword grabbers on the weak side and bottom, and add a cut foam symbol of your own.
  • A felt interior with a pleather outer is a lighter-weight idea one Blade used on a corrugated core.
  • Shield straps: one Blade uses two long straps tied to length, with suede over nylon to keep the look when worn on the back.

Handles

  • A strap handle is the most common. For a punch handle, you need to offset it from the surface, for example with a trowel handle set into the foam (a cheap, imperfect option), or a wooden handle secured with leather straps through the foam and a foam bowl molded around your fist.
  • More reinforcement in the handle makes the shield more responsive. Big washers help spread load.

Covering and sealing

  • Outdoor Mod Podge makes a durable clear coat on a shield.
  • Plasti Dip is mostly used for non-contact surfaces such as pommels and crossguards, but one EVA foam heater shield held up for a year and a half of abuse, including breaking a fall. Plasti Dip also adds texture. Use a comb while it is tacky for a grooved effect.
  • On a latex shield, use latex house paint so the paint does not crack, clean it thoroughly first, and consider a bonding primer. Do not go too heavy, because paint is weight.
  • EVA foam can be scored with a craft knife, textured with a saw blade, and sealed with a heat gun, then painted. Foam that does not melt under aerosols exists, so test spray paint on scrap before you commit.

Pavise

  • A Blade wrote a rudimentary pavise tutorial. It is in the Blades' Google Drive, or ask in #crafting.
  • For a fabric-covered pavise, iron or wash the drop cloth first so the seams do not pull.
  • The stand is the tricky part. Aim for something simple that does not balloon the cost, and test it on a slope, since one early design fell over on an incline.
  • If you need a place to put it, a folding wooden armor stand is a quick build. Honor Before Victory's guide can be done in about three hours.

Hats

Almost everyone makes a big hat. The two main routes are a pleated "battle hat" and a slashed Landsknecht beret.

A simple pleated hat (headband and circle)

  • Measure your forehead. Cut a 4 inch wide rectangle as long as that measurement, fold it in half lengthwise to make a 2 inch band, and sew the ends together into a loop, leaving the long edge open until the end.
  • Cut a circle for the crown. The bigger the diameter, the more the top mushrooms out. One Blade used about 20 inches.
  • Align the headband with the circle edge, pleat the circle to fit, then sew with the pleats held in place. A serger finishes the edges if you have one.

Other construction ideas

  • For a very wide brim, one Blade sandwiched a straw hat brim between three layers of felt and stabilized it with a 19 inch macrame ring, then used a tie or a cap to hold it on. A coif underneath prevents hair squishing.
  • Slashed hats are stressful at first and satisfying after, and the slashes open up when worn. Use felt for the outer layer so you do not need to hem.
  • A linen layer under a felt outer keeps a hat breathable.
  • Feathers add a lot. Hobby Lobby sells cheap white ostrich feathers, and dyed goose feathers are inexpensive online.
  • Patterns that came up: a slashed beret hat pattern on Etsy (one Blade used it), and Thimble and Plume's videos.

Heraldry & Marking Cloth

Official Golden Blades artwork and flags are on the Heraldry page and in the Heraldry & Flags Drive folder. These are ways Blades have put it on gear.

Fabric

  • Textile ink stamps: a steep learning curve. Do not use cardboard as a backing, because the stamp will not fully compress. You only get one shot, and if you lift the stamp before you are sure, you will not find the same spot again and will double the image. Practice on scrap, not on a finished surcoat.
  • Appliqué: put iron-on appliqué backing on first to stop fraying. Be careful stacking two adhesive layers, because ironing the top one can re-activate the bottom one. Double-sided sticky trace paper lets you trace a printed design, stick it to your fabric, cut it out, and stitch around it.
  • Batik: wax blocks where the dye goes (vinyl can guide the wax), then you boil the cloth to melt the wax out. Suggested for banners.
  • Stencils and projectors: project the stencil, trace, then fill in. A paint pen outline hides mistakes.
  • Cricut: works on many fabrics. Some materials will not cut, so one Blade used it only to draw the lines. A rotary tool cuts ironed linen.

Armor and leather

  • You can rotate and resize the sword from the Golden Blades heraldry to fit a piece of armor. Blades have tooled it, painted it, and stamped it. A blue background with the sword and stars in yellow keeps the shape big enough to tool.
  • Texture stamps (for example scales) take practice. Several Blades ended up buying better stamps. Geometric stamps can be made with a 3D printer and a press.

Fonts

  • Grenze (Google Fonts) is a free font Blades use for medieval work.
  • Blambot sells fantasy and display fonts if you want something more unusual.

Leatherwork

Leather is the one craft the Blades have a resident expert for, so ask in #crafting before you buy hides or tools. Most advice below comes from a Blade who makes belts, frogs, bracers, and pouches.

"What do I need to start?"

  • Leather: veg tan in the 7 to 9 oz range is a good start. Buy economy shoulders or sides at Tandy, because there is no need for the premium stuff yet. Chrome tan (for example 5 oz chap sides) is supple and good for pouches.
  • The minimum kit: a scratch awl, a knife (a utility knife with replaceable blades works, and Olfa is available at Lowe's), an edge beveler, a burnisher (a grooved wooden dowel), a hole punch, leather needles, waxed thread, and rivets.
  • The fuller kit: swivel knife, smooth bevel stamps (large, medium, small), tooling weight, maul (a poly maul is comfortable), wing divider, stitch groover, stitching chisels, plastic punch mat, stitching pony, a changeable edge beveler (Keen Edge is recommended), a T-square ruler with non-slip tape on the back, and a rotary punch.
  • A round knife lets you cut with little hand pressure over repeated passes. It is tedious but much easier on your hand than a box cutter, and a flat knife is a handy addition.
  • A "shotgun" style rivet setter is far more comfortable than a normal one, although it can leave a small imprint.
  • Starter sets from Tandy and Weaver are pricey but include a lot. Weaver is considered the higher quality, and Tandy is easier to get to.
  • Tandy staff are knowledgeable and some stores run classes, so ask your local store.
  • Videos: Blades recommend Chuck Dorsett and The Leather Element for starter projects.

Cutting and stitching

  • When swivel-knife cutting, cut only about as deep as the angle of the blade. If you feel you have to push, the leather may be too dry.
  • Rapid rivet rule: if the dented end does not come clearly above the hole, the rivet is too short. If it has a lot of extra room (about 1/8 inch), it is too tall and will set crooked.
  • Chicago screws: measure the stack (for example EVA foam plus leather) and buy the screw whose barrel length matches that, not the advertised length.
  • A saddle stitch is a good fallback when rivets will not hold.
  • For belts that carry load, a heavy lined belt (for example 11 to 12 oz lined with 4 to 5 oz) tilts but does not stretch or sag.
  • Prototype. It is costly to waste leather, but scrap, old shoulders, and cheaper hides are fine for testing a pattern.
  • Making each piece separately (dye, finish, antique, finish) and then assembling is easier than treating the finished item.

Dyeing, painting, and finishing leather

One Blade's order of operations, written for a piece that is to be dyed, painted, and sealed:

  1. Clean the leather. No other prep is needed.
  2. Dye (apply thin, even coats). Leather dye also looks very different outside in sunlight than indoors.
  3. Buff after dye. Wipe off the excess with something absorbent and slightly soft. Mechanic's blue paper towels work well. Buffing is the main defense against dye bleeding. Buff again after paint and after finish.
  4. If you want oil for flexibility, apply it here.
  5. Paint details with leather acrylic paint (Angelus is the recommended brand). Apply thin layers, let each dry, and build up. A heat gun speeds drying. White is not available as a leather dye at Tandy, so Blades use acrylic for white details such as eyes.
  6. Seal with a finish. Fiebing's Resolene diluted 50/50 with water is one Blade's default. Diluted Resolene dries slower, which means fewer streaks and less stripping of acrylics, and it is less shiny, so you can apply several coats over time.
  7. Antique if you like, then finish again.
  • Other finishes are Tan-Kote and Eco-Flo Satin Shene (both at Tandy). Some finishes are designed to be re-applied as they wear, and they differ in how much water protection they give. Resolene is shiny by default.
  • Wet molding works best on undyed leather, but you can spray the back of a dyed piece with water and keep molding.
  • Beware streaks from leather that has not fully dried before you finish it.
  • Calligraphy pen over leather acrylic paint holds, and can be sealed with Resolene.
  • A tinted burnishing medium such as diluted Tokonole, applied with a marker or a chisel-tip pen, helps with edge finishing.

Tooling

  • A smooth beveler pushes the leather beside your cut lines down so the cuts stand out.
  • Gum tragacanth and Tokonole can both be used on edges.
  • Leather is a biological material. Two pieces of the same dye can come out different, and that is part of the charm of leatherwork.

Sword carriage (baldrics and frogs)

  • A baldric for a big two-handed sword can be a shoulder pad with two straps and a hook (a garage hook from the hardware store was used). Tilting the hook toward the back keeps the sword at a good angle for drawing.
  • Tie the belt tail to the sword end to keep it stable when carrying. You untie it before drawing.
  • A leather frog can be laced instead of buckled, which saves the tedious job of buckling each side.

Mail & Scale

  • The Ring Lord sells rings and scales. Blades have built scale pauldrons and chainmail coifs and shirts from their supplies.
  • Solder or weld wire ends closed. Open rings will pry open and come loose when they move against fabric. Backing chain with cloth and sewing it on gives the most security.
  • Mail is a big time sink. Plan on months, not weekends.
  • For ready-made mail, see Shopping, Armor.

Patterns & Sewing

"Where do I get a Landsknecht pattern?"

  • Reconstructing History is the one most Blades use. Their 16th century German collection covers the Landsknecht look. The Blades' pinned picks are RH502 Wams and Hosen, RH503 Waffenrock, and RH506 Doublet and Pluderhose. The Early Tudor gown and jerkin (RH615) also came up.
  • Fair warning: their instructions are unwieldy, and one Blade found the wams fit very wide. Several Blades use them for the theory and then make their own shapes, starting from a T-shirt and sewing cheap-fabric "blanks" to get the shape right.
  • For a dagged coat, search for a collarless generic coat pattern, because "doublet" and "cosplay coat" are what the search engines return. A waffenrock may not be supportive enough for a heavy dagged coat.
  • Etsy has Tudor slashed bias cut patterns and bard shorts patterns. Rabbit and Hat Patterns and Simplicity (S3158 came up) are other sources. Listings change, so search by name.

Free historical resources

  • The Antique Pattern Library has public domain patterns for embroidery, crochet, knitting, sewing, beading, and more.
  • Whilja's Corner (slashed sleeves for a Landsknecht) and Casa de Kissa are blogs Blades recommend.
  • Thimble and Plume is on YouTube and the web.
  • The Medieval Tailor's Assistant is a pattern-drafting reference.

Arming coats and gambesons

  • Padding where it counts and light padding elsewhere helps heat in Texas events. A common build pads the shoulders and keeps the rest thin.
  • Eyelets down the chest, plus eyelets at the shoulders for sleeves and pauldrons, are a popular closure.
  • A gambeson also makes plate more comfortable to wear.

Selling things you make from a pattern

Many pattern makers do not allow it. If you want to sell items made from a pattern, contact the designer first. Some are fine with a small home seller.

Tying and small hardware

  • Cotton cord is a cheap way to mark gear as yours, and braided cord looks fancier.
  • Wrought iron eyelets are sold on Etsy by a maker.

Props & Paper

Aged scrolls and contracts

  • Print on regular paper, crumple it, dip it in tea water, then slowly unfurl it so it does not tear, lay it flat, and dry it with a heat gun. You can lightly burn the edges with a lighter for more age.
  • A calligraphy pen can write on top of acrylic paint on leather, and holds.

Seals and stamps

  • Small wax seal stamp sets are cheap on Etsy, and custom rubber stamps with the House logo can be ordered. Blades have used them for contracts, ribbons, and awards.
  • A Blade keeps a set of contract supplies (stamp, wax, paper) for others to use at the outpost.

3D printing

  • Good first prints are buttons, rings, and small armor plates. Stamps can be printed and pressed too.
  • ASA is stronger than PLA but releases hazardous fumes, so ventilate well.
